The Buffalo Counter

A visualization of the Great Buffalo Collapse, 1800–1900

In 1800, an estimated 60 million buffalo roamed the North American plains. By 1900, fewer than 1,000 remained.

This collapse wasn't natural. Commercial hunting, military campaigns, and government policy destroyed the foundation of Plains Indigenous life on purpose.

The Métis, who depended on buffalo for food, clothing, and trade, saw their world disappear in a single lifetime.

The Hide Trade Begins

Commercial hunting for buffalo robes expands. For every robe that made it to market, several more buffalo were killed and left in the sun. The trade fully obscured the true death toll. The robe trade peaked at 250,000 per year by the 1870s.

Isenberg, 2000
1860
Railroads Reach the Plains🇨🇦

Railroads enable mass slaughter. Hunters fired from train windows, leaving carcasses to rot. The AT&SF depot at Dodge City shipped 200,000 hides per year.

Brown, 1970
1870
The Great Collapse

The population fell from millions to hundreds of thousands. Métis communities faced starvation as the buffalo economy vanished.

Flores, 2016; Peterson, 1985
1874
US Army Campaigns

The strategy was simple: kill the buffalo, break the people. The U.S. Army destroyed the herds to force Indigenous nations onto reservations. Gen. Philip Sheridan championed this policy before Congress.

Congressional Globe, 1874
1883
The Last of the Herds🇨🇦

Fewer than 1,000 buffalo remained in the wild. The species was functionally extinct. Hornaday's 1889 Smithsonian report documented the final toll.

Hornaday, 1889

Population Estimates & Data

  • ↑ Seton, Ernest Thompson. (1909). Life-Histories of Northern Animals, Vol. 1. Charles Scribner's Sons. — Estimated ~60 million buffalo pre-1800.
  • Flores, Dan. (2016). American Serengeti: The Last Big Animals of the Great Plains. University of Oklahoma Press. — Estimated ~30 million; ecological carrying capacity analysis.
  • Roe, Frank Gilbert. (1951). The North American Buffalo: A Critical Study of the Species in Its Wild State. University of Toronto Press. — Comprehensive critical analysis of all population estimates.
  • ↑ Hornaday, William Temple. (1889). "The Extermination of the American Bison." Annual Report of the Smithsonian Institution, pp. 367–548. Government Printing Office. — First systematic count of remaining buffalo; documented the crash.
